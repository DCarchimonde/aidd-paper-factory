\section{Discussion}

This study supports a measurement-oriented interpretation of molecular-machine-learning benchmarks. The primary question was deliberately narrower than whether random, scaffold, temporal, or leakage-aware splitting is universally preferable. We asked whether train--test target-distribution mismatch has an identifiable predictive consequence inside a scaffold-disjoint benchmark when test size and candidate-generation budget are controlled. The answer was conditional. Target balancing did not yield a reproducible performance advantage in any of the 12 classification cells, while all six regression cells improved under the primary single-group acyclic convention. Those regression gains then weakened sharply when the structural semantics of acyclic scaffolds were changed. A separate representation perturbation preserved the inconclusive classification inference but reversed the sign of seven of nine point estimates. Together, these results argue against interpreting benchmark performance as a model-intrinsic quantity.

\subsection{Target balance is a diagnostic variable, not a guarantee of generalization}

The paired candidate-pool design separates the ability to construct a target-balanced partition from the predictive value of that partition. This distinction is important. A small train--test target gap can make a test sample look statistically representative in one dimension, but it says nothing by itself about the chemical regions represented, the local density of analogues, the presence of activity cliffs, or the intended deployment population. The classification results illustrate this directly: a target-balanced candidate was available within the exact-size scaffold candidate space, yet no classification dataset--model cell showed a corrected performance advantage.

Accordingly, the proposed paired procedure should be interpreted as an audit intervention, not as a recommended leaderboard split. In an application where future compounds arrive chronologically or follow a prospective medicinal-chemistry campaign, a temporal or simulated-time design may be scientifically more appropriate \citep{landrum2023simpd}. Where the central concern is similarity leakage, an explicitly leakage-reduced design such as DataSAIL addresses a different and valuable objective \citep{joeres2025datasail}. Our method instead asks a controlled counterfactual question: if scaffold disjointness and test size are held fixed, how much of the apparent benchmark behaviour is associated with target-distribution mismatch?

\subsection{Split-search budget is a benchmark hyperparameter}

An optimized split is not defined only by its objective. It is also defined by how hard the algorithm searches. In the present study, larger candidate pools gave the target-balanced procedure more opportunities to find an extreme low-gap partition. The continuing improvement for ESOL and FreeSolv through 20,000 candidates shows that search budget can materially change the constructed benchmark even when the objective function is unchanged.

This observation is analogous to a familiar problem in predictive modeling: hyperparameter search can overfit a validation criterion when the search space is expanded until a desirable result appears. Benchmark construction can exhibit the same researcher degree of freedom. We therefore audited candidate budgets before predictive outcomes, imposed a stopping rule, froze classification at 300 candidates, and capped the non-converged regression searches at 20,000 candidates. The important point is not that these budgets are universal constants. It is that the search budget should be reported, justified, and frozen as part of the validation protocol.

\subsection{``Scaffold split'' contains structural semantics}

The regression sensitivity exposes an assumption that is often hidden inside a single phrase: ``Bemis--Murcko scaffold split.'' Acyclic molecules have no ring framework, so an implementation must decide what an empty scaffold means. Grouping all acyclic molecules together creates a large structural exclusion rule: if that group is assigned to training, no acyclic molecule can appear in the test set; if assigned to testing, a potentially large fraction of the dataset moves together. Treating acyclic molecules as singletons encodes the opposite assumption that each acyclic structure is a separate scaffold identity.

Neither convention is a universal chemical truth. They answer different questions about what constitutes a novel structural series. The finding that the large ESOL/FreeSolv effects were strongly attenuated under the singleton convention therefore changes the interpretation of the primary regression result. It is not evidence that the main analysis is erroneous; it is evidence that the estimated benefit of target balancing interacts with the structural grouping rule. A reproducible scaffold benchmark should consequently report how acyclic compounds are treated rather than relying on the splitter name alone.

\subsection{Molecular representation is part of the benchmark specification}

The disconnected-component audit leads to a parallel conclusion about chemical identity. Public molecular datasets can encode salts, counterions, complexes, and multi-component records in the SMILES representation. Retaining these components and selecting one dominant fragment are both defensible computational choices, but they are not generally equivalent. In our data, the dominant-fragment mapping changed hundreds of HIV scaffold assignments, materially altered Morgan fingerprints for a subset of records, and created fragment identities associated with conflicting labels.

For that reason, we did not retrospectively replace the primary source-faithful molecular universe. Instead, we froze one deterministic dominant-fragment perturbation as a sensitivity analysis. The result is instructive: all nine affected classification inferences remained inconclusive, yet seven mean-effect signs changed. Thus, a researcher reporting only the direction of a small AUC difference could tell opposite stories under two reasonable representation policies even though the evidence for a reliable difference is absent under both. This finding is consistent with the broader view that chemical identity, endpoint aggregation, and partition definitions belong to the dataset contract supporting a QSAR claim \citep{nael2026dataset}.

\subsection{Implications for responsible molecular benchmarking}

The practical consequence extends beyond statistical tidiness. Molecular benchmark results influence architecture selection, virtual-screening pipelines, compound prioritization, and decisions about which predictions merit experimental follow-up. A small apparent advantage can therefore propagate into GPU expenditure, purchased or synthesized compounds, biochemical assays, and other costly downstream work. If the advantage is contingent on an under-reported benchmark choice, resources may be allocated on evidence that is less stable than the headline score suggests.

A minimum auditable molecular benchmark should therefore record more than a train/test ratio and a random seed. We recommend reporting: (i) the molecular-identity and duplicate policy; (ii) the exact structural grouping rule, including acyclic handling; (iii) the requested and realized test size; (iv) train/test target summaries; (v) scaffold overlap and concentration; (vi) a chemical-similarity diagnostic; (vii) the number of requested and unique partitions; (viii) any split-optimization objective and search budget; and (ix) machine-readable partition manifests or equivalent immutable identifiers. When a conclusion is sensitive to a reasonable alternative protocol, that disagreement is scientifically more informative than selecting whichever protocol produces the preferred score.

This reporting philosophy complements, rather than replaces, ongoing work on realistic out-of-distribution evaluation. Recent studies have shown that partition choice and seed can materially change QSAR performance estimates \citep{li2026partition}, while leakage-aware and temporal-proxy splitters target important deployment-specific failure modes \citep{joeres2025datasail,landrum2023simpd}. The present results add that even within a scaffold-disjoint design, target balance, scaffold semantics, and molecular representation can interact to determine the measured effect.

\section{Limitations}

Several limitations constrain the scope of the conclusions. First, only six public molecular-property datasets were studied. The 20 partition seeds quantify robustness within these fixed molecular universes and are not 20 independent draws from a population of chemical datasets. Second, the predictive models are classical Morgan-fingerprint baselines rather than graph neural networks or molecular foundation models. This was intentional to reduce architecture-related variability, but future work should test whether the same protocol interactions persist across learned representations.

Third, target balancing controls only the first moment of the response distribution and exact test size. It does not explicitly match target variance, tails, mechanistic subgroups, or local chemical density. Fourth, candidate generation is a stochastic prefix heuristic rather than an exhaustive enumeration of all feasible scaffold subsets. The regression budget audit did not demonstrate convergence by 20,000 candidates, so the reported regression partitions should be interpreted at that frozen search budget rather than as global optima. Fifth, the single-group and singleton acyclic conventions are two operational extremes, not an exhaustive ontology of acyclic chemical series.

Sixth, the dominant-fragment sensitivity is deliberately algorithmic. The largest heavy-atom fragment is not guaranteed to be the biologically correct parent of a salt, mixture, co-crystal, or metal complex, and no claim of chemical standardization optimality is made. Finally, no chronological, prospective, or external industrial test set was available. The study diagnoses benchmark construction; it does not establish which internal split best predicts prospective drug-discovery performance.

\section{Conclusion}

A molecular benchmark is not merely a dataset divided into training and test rows; it is a measurement instrument constructed from choices about molecular identity, structural grouping, partition search, and statistical replication. In an exact-size paired scaffold audit across six molecular-property datasets, reducing train--test target mismatch did not yield a reproducible classification advantage, whereas strong regression improvements appeared under one acyclic-scaffold convention and weakened substantially under another. A deterministic dominant-fragment perturbation preserved the absence of supported classification differences while reversing most point-estimate directions.

These findings show that split-induced performance effects are protocol-conditioned. The appropriate response is not to search for one universally superior split, but to state the validation question, freeze the benchmark-construction rules, preserve partition provenance, and test whether the scientific claim survives reasonable perturbations of those rules. Treating benchmark construction as part of the chemometric measurement process can reduce false confidence in model comparisons and provide a more reliable basis for decisions that move computational predictions toward costly experimental evaluation.
