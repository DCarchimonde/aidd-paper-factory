\begin{abstract}
Molecular-machine-learning benchmarks are commonly treated as neutral measurement devices, although their reported scores can depend on how molecules are represented, grouped, and assigned to validation partitions. We present a paired chemometric audit that isolates one such design factor: train--test target-distribution mismatch under scaffold-disjoint evaluation. Six public molecular-property datasets (BACE, BBBP, ClinTox, HIV, ESOL, and FreeSolv) were rebuilt with explicit molecular-identity and duplicate policies. For each of 20 pre-specified partition seeds, a fixed budget of target-blind scaffold candidates was generated. A size-matched scaffold baseline was selected without target information, and a target-balanced counterpart was selected only from candidates with exactly the same test-set size. Candidate-search budgets, partition hashes, and statistical units were frozen before model fitting. Logistic/ridge regression, random forests, and XGBoost models were evaluated with Morgan fingerprints.

Across the 12 classification dataset--model cells, no target-balanced performance advantage remained supported after paired bootstrap analysis, Wilcoxon testing, and Holm correction. In contrast, all six regression cells favored target balancing under the primary convention that all acyclic molecules share one scaffold identity; mean RMSE reductions ranged from 0.105 to 1.149. This apparent regression advantage was strongly attenuated when acyclic molecules were treated as singleton scaffold groups, with FreeSolv point estimates reversing direction. A separate dominant-fragment sensitivity analysis for BBBP, ClinTox, and HIV left all nine classification inferences inconclusive, although seven of nine mean-effect directions changed sign relative to the source-faithful representation.

These results show that target-distribution control can change benchmark composition without guaranteeing a universal predictive benefit, and that apparent split effects can depend on structural grouping semantics and molecular-record representation. Molecular benchmark performance should therefore be interpreted as protocol-conditioned evidence rather than as a model-intrinsic quantity. We provide a reproducible audit framework based on exact-size paired perturbations, fixed search budgets, hashed partition manifests, and partition-level inference.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} molecular property prediction; chemometrics; benchmark validation; scaffold split; target distribution; data splitting; molecular standardization; reproducibility
