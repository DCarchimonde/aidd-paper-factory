\section{Introduction}

A predictive benchmark is often treated as a neutral ruler: a model is trained, a held-out score is calculated, and differences between scores are interpreted as differences in model capability. In chemometrics, however, the validity of that interpretation depends on how the validation sample was constructed. Resampling can change the population being measured, the degree of extrapolation required, and the uncertainty attached to the reported estimate. Principles of proper model validation therefore emphasize that the test design is part of the measurement process rather than a clerical step after model fitting \citep{esbensen2010proper,tropsha2003earnest,gramatica2007principles,lasfar2024robustness,kiraly2025leakage,camacho2026validation}.

Molecular property prediction makes this dependence especially visible. Resources such as MoleculeNet standardized a collection of widely used classification and regression tasks \citep{wu2018moleculenet}, while advances in molecular representations and learning algorithms have driven extensive leaderboard comparisons. Yet a molecular benchmark score remains conditional on the protocol used to define an unseen molecule. Random partitions can place close chemical neighbours in both training and test sets. Bemis--Murcko scaffold splitting \citep{bemis1996properties} instead withholds structural frameworks and is widely used as a proxy for transfer to new chemical series. The resulting partition is not, however, characterized by scaffold overlap alone. It can also change the response distribution, the concentration of scaffold groups, local chemical similarity, and the number of observations assigned to the test set.

These concerns are increasingly recognized. Comparative studies have shown substantial variation across random and scaffold partitions and have questioned simple claims of chemical-space generalization from a single benchmark score \citep{yang2019learned,deng2023systematic}. Leakage-aware splitters such as DataSAIL explicitly optimize structural separation to obtain more realistic out-of-distribution evaluation \citep{joeres2025datasail}, while SIMPD constructs simulated time splits to approximate prospective medicinal-chemistry evaluation \citep{landrum2023simpd}. Recent QSAR analyses similarly show that partition method and random seed can materially affect internal performance and need not predict external generalizability \citep{li2026partition}. At the dataset level, chemical identity rules, endpoint aggregation, standardization, and split definitions have also been framed as components of the scientific claim itself rather than as interchangeable implementation details \citep{nael2026dataset}. Together, these studies establish that benchmark construction matters.

A narrower question remains difficult to answer from comparisons in which several partition properties change simultaneously: \emph{what is the contribution of train--test target-distribution mismatch within an otherwise scaffold-disjoint benchmark?} If an ordinary scaffold split has both a different test size and a different target distribution from an alternative split, a change in predictive score cannot be attributed cleanly to either factor. Likewise, if a target-informed splitter searches a much larger partition space than its reference procedure, the search budget itself becomes an unreported degree of freedom. Repeating model fits under duplicated partitions can further create pseudoreplication by treating algorithmic randomness as if it represented independent test populations.

We address this problem as a controlled chemometric perturbation of benchmark construction. For each dataset and pre-specified partition seed, we generate a fixed-budget pool of target-blind, scaffold-disjoint candidate partitions. A size-matched baseline is selected without using the target. The target-balanced counterpart is then selected only from candidates with \emph{exactly the same test-set size}, minimizing the absolute difference between training and test target means. The paired partitions therefore share the dataset, scaffold definition, candidate-generation budget, and test size; the designed contrast is the degree of target-distribution balance. The procedure is used as an audit, not as a claim that a target-balanced split is intrinsically more realistic or should replace chronological, prospective, or application-specific validation.

The study further tests whether the observed performance contrast survives two chemically meaningful representation choices. First, the Bemis--Murcko scaffold of an acyclic molecule is empty under the usual framework definition. Treating all such molecules as one scaffold group imposes a different structural semantics from treating each acyclic molecule as a singleton group. We therefore compare these conventions for the regression tasks. Second, public benchmark records can contain disconnected salts, counterions, complexes, or other multi-component representations. A deterministic dominant-fragment perturbation is used to test whether classification conclusions depend on retaining the source-faithful multi-component record. The dominant fragment is explicitly treated as an algorithmic sensitivity representation, not as an assertion of the chemically correct parent structure.

Four methodological contributions follow. First, we formulate an exact-size paired target-balance scaffold audit that isolates target-distribution mismatch more tightly than a direct comparison of unrelated splitters. Second, we audit and freeze the candidate-search budget before predictive outcomes are examined, making benchmark optimization itself an explicit experimental factor. Third, every partition is stored in a molecule-level manifest and identified by a cryptographic hash; inference is performed on unique paired partitions rather than on model seeds. Fourth, we examine two interactions that are usually hidden inside the phrase ``scaffold split'': acyclic scaffold semantics and disconnected-component representation.

The practical motivation is not to increase a leaderboard score. Molecular benchmark results increasingly influence which modeling approaches are trusted for virtual screening, compound prioritization, and downstream experimental evaluation. If an apparent performance advantage is contingent on an under-reported split or representation choice, computational and experimental resources can be allocated on unstable evidence. A benchmark should therefore be viewed as a measurement instrument whose construction must be specified and audited. Our aim is to identify which molecular-machine-learning conclusions remain stable after that instrument is deliberately perturbed.
